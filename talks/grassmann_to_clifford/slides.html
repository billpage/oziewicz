How the notes keep faith with it

  1. State each claim in its strongest form before testing it.
  2. Check the mathematics, not allegiance: does the identity hold, does the construction compose, does the statement follow?
  3. Report the result either way: textbook physics in new notation, or a conflict that survives.

Where the method parts from him

He held that science needs no criterion for who is right. A repository that checks derivations does not share that view, and it does not claim that he would have agreed with it.

Today's example: From Grassmann to Clifford (1986)

The thesis in three claims

  1. Exterior multiplication e and its transpose i are fermion creation and annihilation operators, with no metric: \(\{i_{\V x}, e_{\C\alpha}\} = \C\alpha\V x\).
  2. A Clifford generator is a “quasi-particle”: \(\gamma_{\V v} = e_{\V v} + i_{\G\phi \V v}\), a creator plus an annihilator.
  3. The Clifford product lives on the same space as the Grassmann algebra. The map \(\G\phi\) from vectors to covectors need not be symmetric: \(\G\phi = \G g + \G\omega\).

Where the talk ends up

What is given without any choice is the evaluation \(\C\alpha\V x\) of a covector on a vector. The Grassmann algebra is its Fock space. A metric is the choice of a subspace: the graph of \(\G\phi\).

Z. Oziewicz, in J. S. R. Chisholm and A. K. Common (eds.), Clifford Algebras and Their Applications in Mathematical Physics, NATO ASI C 183, Reidel 1986, pp. 245–255.

Two spaces, neither first

A covector \(\C\alpha\) is its kernel plus one vector on which it reads 1. Its value \(\C\alpha\V x\) is the number of sheets the arrow pierces: no length, no angle.

A metric identifies L with L* and hides the difference. The paper insists that not choosing a metric is not the same as having none available.

Where it stops

A rod that measures \(|v|\) is not linear. Lengths need one more instrument, of another kind: that is where \(\G\phi\) enters.

What makes an instrument a covector: linear response, blind directions (a whole hyperplane, \(\ker\C\alpha\)), and one calibration. analysis

Grassmann's products, defined by evaluation

The pairing by determinants defines both wedge products at once, with no factorial and no metric:

\[(\C{\alpha^1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\C{\alpha^k})(\V{x_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge \V{x_k}) := \det\big[\C{\alpha^i}\V{x_j}\big] \qquad (3)\]

Exterior multiplication, and its transpose:

\[e_x\,y := x\wedge y,\qquad e_x\circ e_y = e_{x\wedge y}\qquad (7)\] \[(i_x\omega)(y) := \omega(x\wedge y),\qquad i_x\circ i_y = i_{y\wedge x}\qquad (9),\,(12)\]

The order reverses: \(i\) is an anti-homomorphism, because transposition reverses products.

Example: the usual contraction

\[i_x(\alpha\wedge\beta) = (\alpha x)\,\beta - (\beta x)\,\alpha\]

Antiderivation, eq. (13)

\[i_x\circ e_\alpha = e_{i_x\alpha} + (-1)^{\deg\alpha}\,e_\alpha\circ i_x\]

Textbooks (Greub) take this as the definition of \(i_x\). Here it is a theorem: the Laplace expansion of a determinant.

checked (6), (7), (12), (13), in dimensions 3 and 4

Fermions without a metric

The super-bracket of operators of degrees \(\deg A, \deg B\):

\[\{A,B\} := A\circ B - (-1)^{\deg A\,\deg B}\,B\circ A\qquad (14)\]

For a vector \(\V x\) and covectors \(\C\alpha, \C\beta\), acting on forms:

\[\{e_{\C\alpha}, e_{\C\beta}\} = 0,\quad \{i_{\V x}, i_{\V y}\} = 0,\quad \{i_{\V x}, e_{\C\alpha}\} = \C\alpha\V x\qquad (16)\]

The canonical anticommutation relations, with no Hilbert space and no metric: the right-hand side is the duality pairing. checked

Pairs are not ideal bosons, eq. (17)

\[[\,i_{x\wedge y},\, e_\alpha\,] = \alpha(x\wedge y) + e_{i_y\alpha}\circ i_x - e_{i_x\alpha}\circ i_y\]

The first term is the bosonic one; the other two are the corrections.

\(e_{\V x}\) raises the degree: creation \(i_{\C\alpha}\) lowers it: annihilation \(i_{\C\alpha}1 = 0\)

The Grassmann algebras of multivectors and of forms are Fock spaces, in the algebraic sense of the next slide: if \(e\) creates “fermions”, \(i = e^{*}\) annihilates “antifermions”.

“Fock space” here: an algebraic structure

Definition

For dual spaces \(L, L^*\): a vector space \(F\) with a vacuum \(1\in F\), creators \(e_{\V x}\) for \(\V x\in L\) and annihilators \(i_{\C\alpha}\) for \(\C\alpha\in L^*\), with

\[i_{\C\alpha}1 = 0,\qquad \{i_{\C\alpha},e_{\V x}\} = \C\alpha\V x,\] \[\{e_{\V x},e_{\V y}\} = 0,\qquad \{i_{\C\alpha},i_{\C\beta}\} = 0,\]

and \(F\) spanned by the \(e_{x_1}\cdots e_{x_k}1\).

Then \(F\cong{\textstyle\bigwedge} L\), with \(e\) the wedge and \(i\) the contraction: the space is unique, and every operator on it is a polynomial in the \(e\)'s and \(i\)'s, \(C\ell(L\oplus L^*)\cong\End{\textstyle\bigwedge}L\). analysis

Finite: \(\dim F = 2^n\). Any field of scalars.

Quasi-particles: the doubled space

A creator plus an annihilator, acting on multivectors:

\[(e_{\V x} + i_{\C\alpha})^2 = \C\alpha\V x\qquad (22)\]

So \((\V x,\C\alpha)\mapsto e_{\V x}+i_{\C\alpha}\) is a Clifford map for the neutral form \(Q(\V x,\C\alpha) = \C\alpha\V x\) on the doubled space \(\cV^1\oplus\Lambda^1\): the standard isomorphism \(C\ell(L\oplus L^*)\cong \End{\textstyle\bigwedge} L\). analysis

Now choose a linear map \(\G\phi:\cV^1\to\Lambda^1\) and stay on its graph:

\[\gamma_{\V v} := e_{\V v} + i_{\G\phi\V v},\qquad \gamma_{\V v}^2 = (\G\phi \V v)(\V v) = \G g(\V v,\V v)\]

The quadratic form of the Clifford algebra is evaluation, restricted to the graph of \(\G\phi\).

\(\cV^1\), vectors = graph of 0: the Grassmann algebra \(\Lambda^1\), covectors graph of \(\phi\) \((\V v,\, \C{\phi v})\) \(v\) \(\phi v\) schematic: the doubled space has dimension 2n, a graph dimension n

The paper also defines a product on \(\cV\oplus\Lambda\) itself, eqs. (19)–(20), its “generalized Kähler–Atiyah algebra”. It is not associative, and it is not the one that survives.

The Clifford product, eq. (23)

\(\gamma:\cV\to\End\cV\) is fixed by three conditions:

\[\gamma_v = e_v\ \ (\deg v = 0),\qquad \gamma_v = e_v + i_{\G\phi v}\ \ (\deg v = 1),\] \[\gamma_{\gamma_v w} = \gamma_v\circ\gamma_w\quad\text{(associativity)}\]

The product \(u\cdot w := \gamma_u w\) lives on the space \(\cV\) of the Grassmann algebra. For a vector \(v\): \(v\cdot w = v\wedge w + i_{\G\phi v}w\). Recursion, for vectors \(v, w\):

\[\gamma_{v\wedge w} = \gamma_v\gamma_w - \G\phi(v,w)\qquad (25)\]

Split \(\G\phi = \G g + \G\omega\), symmetric plus antisymmetric:

\[\{\gamma_v,\gamma_w\} = 2\,\G g(v,w),\] \[\gamma_{v\wedge w} = \tfrac12[\gamma_v,\gamma_w] - \G\omega(v,w)\qquad (26)\]

checked (23) for a generic non-symmetric \(\G\phi\), all pairs of basis blades, \(n = 3, 4\); (24)–(27)

The anticommutator sees only \(\G g\).

\(\G\omega\) changes which element of the algebra is called the bivector \(v\wedge w\).

Existence is asserted, not proved

Uniqueness follows from the recursion. Existence is Chevalley's theorem (1954). Oziewicz constructs the product himself in 1997.

What 1986 hoped for

\(\G g\) gravitational and \(\G\omega\) electromagnetic, after Einstein's non-symmetric unified theory; \(\G\omega\) as a “distortion of spinor space”.

The antisymmetric part is a gauge

Contraction by the 2-form \(\G\omega\), \(i_{\G\omega}\), annihilates a pair. Its exponential (Jadczyk 2021, Prop. 3.10, in 1986 notation):

\[e^{i_{\omega}}\,e_x\,e^{-i_{\omega}} = e_x + i_{\omega x},\qquad e^{i_{\omega}}\,i_\alpha\,e^{-i_{\omega}} = i_\alpha\] \[e^{i_{\omega}}(u\cdot_{g} w) = (e^{i_{\omega}}u)\cdot_{g+\omega}(e^{i_{\omega}}w),\] \[e^{i_{\omega}}(v\wedge w) = v\wedge w + \omega(v,w)\]

So \((\cV,\,\cdot\,)\cong C\ell(\cV^1, \G g)\): the algebra depends only on \(\G g\). checked generic \(g,\omega\); \(n = 3, 4\)

Why: an isometry of the doubled space analysis

The shear \((\V x,\C\alpha)\mapsto(\V x,\C\alpha+\G\omega \V x)\) keeps \(Q\), because \(\G\omega(x,x) = 0\). It is implemented by \(e^{i_{\omega}}\). The shear by \(\G g\) changes \(Q\) by \(\G g(x,x)\): the algebra really changes.

A sign: under his own conventions (60)–(63), Jadczyk's eq. (68) should read \([\bar i_{A^*},\bar e_x] = -\bar i^{A}_x\). Oziewicz's convention (12) is the one for which it holds as printed. checked

What \(\omega\) does change: the grading

The dispute over the wedge

Hestenes and Sobczyk define the wedge as the antisymmetrized Clifford product:

\[\gamma_{v\wedge w} = \tfrac12[\gamma_v,\gamma_w]\iff \omega = 0\]

Hestenes fixes the gauge \(\omega = 0\); Oziewicz declines to. Mathematically, that is the whole disagreement. checked

The bundle picture (Jadczyk §3.2)

\(Q = 0\) \(Q = g\) \(\bigwedge V\) \(C\ell(g)\) symmetric: moves \(\omega\) base: quadratic forms

An alternating form acts within a fibre: the gauge. 1986 sits over \(Q = 0\) and pulls the product back from the fibre over \(g\).

Consequences for 1986

  • All the \(\omega\)-dependence of the idempotents (31) is a relabelling. checked
  • No “distortion of spinor space” at a point.
  • Einstein's \(\omega\) can matter only through its derivatives.
  • Fauser and Abłamowicz: “Clifford algebra of a bilinear form” names the pair (algebra, grading).

\(g\) gives the algebra; \(\omega\) gives the grading, that is, which elements count as \(k\)-vectors.

Idempotents, spinors and signature

Why impose associativity at all? The paper's answer: spinors, as a minimal left ideal \(\cV\cdot p\) for a primitive idempotent \(p\), which need not exist globally. Try

\[2p = \lambda + v + a\wedge b,\qquad \lambda\in\mathcal F,\ \ v,a,b\in\cV^1\qquad (30)\]

Then \(p\cdot p = p\), \(p\neq 1\), if and only if (with \(a\wedge b\neq 0\))

\[v\wedge a\wedge b = 0,\qquad \lambda = 1+\omega(a,b),\] \[g(v,v)+g(a,b)^2-g(a,a)\,g(b,b) = 1\]

The third condition printed in (31) is redundant: it follows from the first. checked For \(g = 0\) there are none: a Grassmann algebra has only the idempotents 0 and 1.

Primitive? It depends on the signature analysis

The paper's 4-dimensional claim needs \(g(v,v) = 1\), \(v\perp a, b\), and a plane \(a,b\) of signature (1,1).

(−,+,+,+): possible. \(C\ell_{3,1}\cong M_4(\mathbb R)\), and the product idempotent has rank 4, a minimal ideal. checked

(+,−,−,−): impossible; \(v^\perp\) is negative definite. \(C\ell_{1,3}\cong M_2(\mathbb H)\), and \(\tfrac12(1+v)\) is already primitive.

The exterior derivative, §9

\(d\) does not preserve a spinor ideal: true. On \(\mathbb R^2\), \(\beta = \tfrac12(1+dx)\): \((\delta_\beta d - d\,\delta_\beta) f = \tfrac12\partial_x f\). But the paper's bracket is for left multiplication, its conclusion for right.

The sequel: Clifford algebra of multivectors (1997)

Quantization

A family of ideals in the tensor algebra \(TM\), and quotients \(C\ell(M,\eta) = TM/\ker\pi(\eta)\): space and product both vary. A “bivector” means nothing there.

Deformation

One fixed space of multivectors \(M^\wedge\), with a family of products \(\wedge^\eta\): the Clifford algebra of multivectors.

The bridge (Cor. 3.2, a form of PBW)

\[TM/\mu I\;\cong\;\lambda TM/I\]

\(\lambda,\mu\) are mutually inverse bijections of tensors: Jadczyk's \(\lambda_F\) and \(\lambda_{-F}\).

  • Existence of the 1986 product is proved (Theorems 4.2, 4.3). His polynomials (6.6), (6.7) reduce to 1986 (27). checked
  • He settles \(\omega\) himself: it labels isomorphic algebras. The bare grading is broken, and a “clouded” one kept.
  • Physical reading (credited to Fauser): \(\eta\) is the anticommutator, \(\omega\) the propagator. Normal ordering is a triangular \(\phi\).
  • New: any pre-braid in place of the sign rule; the opposite algebra, \(u\wedge^{\phi}w = (-1)^{|u||w|}\,w\wedge^{-\phi^T}u\). checked

does not hold as written

A unique clouded \(\mathbb Z\)-grading for every \(\phi\): \(v\wedge^\phi v = \eta(v,v)\) is a non-zero scalar. With \(\eta\neq 0\) the algebra is only \(\mathbb Z_2\)-graded and filtered. checked

Dropped since 1986: the forms \(\Lambda\), the doubled space, idempotents, \(d\).

Conjecture 8.2 holds in every dimension

With \(\xi = \eta^{-1}\), acting on \(M^\wedge\otimes M^\wedge\), \(n = \dim M\), the 1997 paper conjectures that \(\Gamma\) has the minimum polynomial \(p_n\), whatever \(\eta\) is:

\[\Gamma := \xi^{\mu\nu}\,\gamma_\mu\otimes\gamma_\nu,\qquad p_n(x) = \prod_{k=0}^{n}\big(x-(n-2k)\big)\]

Reported there as proved for \(n\le 3\) by hand and for \(n = 4\) in Maple (A. R. Rodríguez Domínguez). analysis Proof for all \(n\): take an \(\eta\)-orthogonal basis, \(\epsilon_\mu = \eta(e_\mu,e_\mu)\), and

\[T_\mu := \epsilon_\mu^{-1}\,(\gamma_\mu\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_\mu),\qquad T_\mu^2 = 1,\qquad T_\mu T_\nu = T_\nu T_\mu\]

So \(\Gamma = \sum_\mu T_\mu\) is a sum of \(n\) commuting involutions. Every pattern of signs occurs, because the representation is faithful. The eigenvalues are exactly \(n, n-2, \dots, -n\). \(\blacksquare\)

checked \(n = 1,\dots,5\); and \(n = 4\) in signatures (1,3), (2,2), non-diagonal \(\eta\), non-symmetric \(\phi\)

Multiplicity \(\binom nk 2^n\): the total “spin” \(\sum_\mu\sigma_z^{(\mu)}\) of \(n\) two-state systems, each asking whether the two factors agree in direction \(\mu\).

Multiplicities: numerical (eigenvalues of integer matrices, \(n\le 5\)). This is the algebra of Fierz rearrangement; the result is probably known there. I have not searched.

Global duality keeps Oziewicz's rule that a space and its dual are never identified. Local duality is a metric on one space: inside the doubled space, \(c_i = a_i + \tfrac12\sum_{j\neq i} b_j\). checked

The thesis: three verdicts

1 · Proved

For every linear \(\G\phi\), the Grassmann space with \(\gamma_v = e_v + i_{\G\phi v}\) carries an associative product. Postulated in 1986, constructed by Oziewicz in 1997, proved in full by Chevalley, Bourbaki and Jadczyk, checked here for generic \(\G\phi\).

2 · Reduced

Only the symmetric part \(\G g\) determines the algebra. \(\G\omega\) is a gauge that chooses the grading. So \(\G\phi\) is over-parametrised, and Oziewicz says so himself in 1997.

3 · Not a theorem

“Primary” is a claim of priority: one can start from Clifford and get Grassmann as \(\G g = 0\). What can be defended: the abstract Clifford algebra does not know what a bivector is. The pair (algebra, grading) does, and the Grassmann structure is that extra datum.

The thesis in its sharper form

What is given without any choice is the evaluation of covectors on vectors. The Grassmann algebra is its Fock space. A metric is the choice of a subspace, the graph of \(\G\phi\), to which evaluation is restricted.

Also corrected: the 1986 “symplectic Clifford algebra” (\(\G g = 0\)) is the Grassmann algebra again, not the Weyl algebra, which deforms the symmetric algebra. 1997 puts it there.

Open questions

Fields, not points

In 1986 \(\phi\), \(\omega\) and \(p\) are fields; evaluation is the same at every point. Is the natural setting the doubled bundle \(T\oplus T^*\), with \(g\) and \(\omega\) a field of graphs, and \(d\omega\) the obstruction in §9?

The symmetric twin

Permanents in place of determinants in (3), commutators in (16): does the same construction give the Weyl algebra, with the roles of \(g\) and \(\omega\) exchanged?

The null substrate

Is the simplex metric of local duality singled out by anything in the evaluation structure, or only by its symmetry? Does anything in the programme depend on \(\omega\)?

Braids

1997 allows any pre-braid. Is \(\omega\) still an isometry of a braided doubled space? Is \(\Gamma\) still a sum of commuting operators with two eigenvalues each?

And one for the literature: where is Conjecture 8.2 already known? The Fierz identities are the place to look.
