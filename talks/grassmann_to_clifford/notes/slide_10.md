I've used the words "Fock space", "fermion" and "vacuum", and they can mislead, because to most physicists they mean quantum mechanics: a Hilbert space, states, particles and probabilities. Here they mean something purely algebraic, and since the whole talk leans on that phrase, I want to be precise about it.

On the left is the definition I have in mind. Take a vector space L and its dual. A Fock space for that pair is a vector space F with one distinguished element, the vacuum, together with creation operators e of x, one for each vector x, and annihilation operators i of α, one for each covector α. The annihilators kill the vacuum. Two creators anticommute, two annihilators anticommute, and the anticommutator of an annihilator with a creator is the number α of x. And F is spanned by what the creators make from the vacuum.

That is all. It follows that F is the Grassmann algebra of L, with e the wedge and i the contraction, so the space is unique up to isomorphism. And every linear operator on it is a polynomial in the e's and i's, which is the isomorphism between the Clifford algebra of L plus its dual and the operators on the Grassmann algebra that we'll meet on the next slide. The space is finite, of dimension 2 to the n, and the scalars can be any field.

The table compares this with the fermion Fock space of quantum mechanics. Two rows matter most. In quantum mechanics, creators and annihilators are labelled by the same space, H, and their anticommutator is the inner product. Here they are labelled by two different spaces, a space and its dual, and the anticommutator is the pairing between them. Correspondingly, i is obtained from e by transposition, not by a Hermitian adjoint. And no physical meaning is assumed at all: "fermion" is just the name of the algebra that these relations define.

The box at the bottom says how the two meet. Choose an inner product, which is to say identify L with its dual by a metric, and contraction by the dual of x becomes the adjoint of e of x. That gives the fermion Fock space of quantum mechanics. The algebraic Fock space is what is there before that choice. So, once again, the step from the algebraic to the familiar is the choice of a metric.
