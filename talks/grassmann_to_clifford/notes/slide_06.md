The paper begins conceptually, with a vector space L and its dual L star. Each is the space of linear functions on the other, and algebra alone does not tell them apart. But they are never canonically identified.

The figure, which is from the supplement, shows the picture the paper suggests. In panel (a), a vector x is an arrow and a covector α is a stack of parallel sheets, its level sets. The two thick sheets are the kernel of α and the sheet where α equals one: that is the paper's description of a covector as a hyperplane plus one vector on which it takes the value 1. The value α of x is the number of sheets the arrow pierces, here two and a half. The vector y lies along the sheets, so α of y is zero. Notice that no length and no angle was used.

Panel (b) exchanges the roles. In the dual space, α is the arrow and x is the stack of sheets, and the count is the same. That is why the two form a pair: neither picture comes first.

Panel (c) is the same idea for fields. A vector field X is drawn by its flow lines, and a 1-form α by its wave fronts. The integral of α along the path is the number of fronts crossed, here four, and the function α of X is the rate at which the flow crosses the fronts. Vector fields generate flows; forms are what one integrates. I should add that panel (c) draws an exact form, and a general 1-form has wave fronts only locally.

The paper's main point here is on the right. Choosing a metric identifies L with its dual and hides the difference between them. And it insists that not choosing a metric is something different from having none available. That distinction runs through everything that follows.
