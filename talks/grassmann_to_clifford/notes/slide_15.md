Eleven years later, Oziewicz wrote the sequel: Clifford algebra of multivectors, in Advances in Applied Clifford Algebras, 1997. I read it in a later re-typeset version that he made himself. It does not cite the 1986 paper, but it is the same programme with the proofs moved to the tensor algebra, and it is the paper Jadczyk names as his starting point.

Its organising distinction is between quantization and deformation. Quantization is a family of ideals in the tensor algebra, and so a family of quotient algebras whose underlying spaces and products both vary. In such a quotient, a "bivector" has no meaning. Deformation is a family of products on one fixed space, the multivectors: that is what he means by the Clifford algebra of multivectors. The bridge between them is a pair of mutually inverse linear maps of tensors, λ and μ. Quantizing by μ is the same as deforming by λ, which he presents as a form of the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem. His λ and μ are exactly Jadczyk's λ F and λ minus F.

What this gives the 1986 paper: existence of the product is now constructed, and his explicit polynomials reduce to equation 27 of 1986. That was checked. He settles ω himself: it labels isomorphic algebras. He says the original, "bare" grading is broken by the deformation, and a "clouded" grading is kept. He credits Fauser with the physical reading: η, his symmetric part, is the anticommutator, and ω is the propagator. Normal ordering is the case of a triangular φ. And there are new things: any pre-braid in place of the sign rule, and the opposite algebra, which is also checked.

One claim cannot stand as written. He says that for every φ there is a unique clouded integer grading preserved by the product. But v times v is η of v, v, a non-zero scalar, where a graded product would put it in degree two. With η not zero, the algebra is only Z 2 graded and filtered. The statement holds for η equal to zero, and is presumably a slip of generality.
