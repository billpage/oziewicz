Why does the paper insist on associativity at all? Its answer is spinors. A spinor space is a minimal left ideal, the algebra times a primitive idempotent p. The paper stresses that p is a multivector field that need not exist globally, and offers that as the reason spinors do not exist on every manifold.

It then tries a family of idempotents, equation 30: twice p equals a scalar λ, plus a vector v, plus a bivector a wedge b. Squaring and comparing grades, p is idempotent, and not one, exactly when v wedge a wedge b is zero, λ is one plus ω of a, b, and the last condition on the slide holds. The paper prints a third condition on vectors, but it is redundant: it follows from the first. That was checked. And for g equal to zero there are no such idempotents, which is as it should be, since a Grassmann algebra has only the idempotents zero and one.

The paper then makes a claim about four-dimensional space-time: that a product of two commuting idempotents of this kind is primitive, fixed by a complete moving frame. This depends on the signature, which the paper does not state. It needs a unit vector v orthogonal to a plane of signature one, one. In signature minus, plus, plus, plus, that is possible; the Clifford algebra is the 4 by 4 real matrices, and the product idempotent has rank four, which is a minimal ideal. In signature plus, minus, minus, minus, it is impossible: v is timelike, its orthogonal complement is negative definite, and in that algebra one half of one plus v is already primitive.

The last section of the paper, on the bottom right, concludes that the exterior derivative does not commute with projection onto a spinor ideal. That is true, already in the flat plane, and the slide gives a two-line example. But the paper's argument proves a bracket for left multiplication and draws its conclusion for right multiplication, so it is a plausibility argument, not a derivation.
