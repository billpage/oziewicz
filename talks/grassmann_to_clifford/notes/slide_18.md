Now to Garret Sobczyk's null-vector programme. Garret is thanked in the 1986 acknowledgements, and his book with Hestenes is one of the paper's targets, so the comparison seemed natural. None of the passages I read cites the 1986 paper; the connections are mine.

The table is a dictionary. Garret's globally dual Grassmann algebras impose the relation a i b j plus b j a i equals delta i j on two families of null vectors. In 1986 terms, a i is exterior multiplication by a vector, b j is contraction by a covector, and the relation is equation 16. Oziewicz derives it; Garret imposes it. In both, the null generators are fermion creators and annihilators. Garret's e i equal to a i plus b i, and f i equal to a i minus b i, are the quasi-particle operators with φ equal to plus or minus the identity. His algebra G n, n, the 2 to the n by 2 to the n real matrices, is the algebra of all operators on the Grassmann algebra.

Locally dual null vectors are different. They are one Grassmann algebra with one particular symmetric φ, on n plus 1 vectors with g of c i, c j equal to a half for i different from j. That metric has signature one, n. And the observer idempotent in Garret's keynote draft is, in 1986 notation, exterior multiplication by T composed with contraction by τ, with τ of T equal to one: a fermion number operator.

Two differences. First, a hidden choice of ω. Definition 4 of the 2026 paper by Garret, Jesús Cruz Guzmán and me takes the wedge to be the antisymmetrized product, which is φ equal to g. Then c 1 c 2 is c 1 wedge c 2 plus a half. But the index arithmetic on ordered products works with a triangular φ, which has a non-zero ω, and for which ordered products are wedge blades. It is the same algebra with two different gradings, so statements about bivectors should say which one is meant. Second, global duality keeps Oziewicz's rule that a space is never identified with its dual. Local duality does not: it is a metric on one space. Inside the doubled space, c i is a i plus a half the sum of the other b's.
