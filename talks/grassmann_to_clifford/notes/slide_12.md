This is the central definition of the paper, equation 23. The map gamma, from multivectors to operators on multivectors, is fixed by three conditions. On scalars, gamma is just multiplication. On vectors, it is the quasi-particle operator, e of v plus i of φ v. And it must be associative: gamma of gamma v of w equals gamma v composed with gamma w.

The Clifford product of two multivectors, u dot w, is then gamma of u applied to w. It lives on the same space as the Grassmann algebra. For a vector v, v dot w is v wedge w plus the contraction of w by φ v.

From associativity comes a recursion, equation 25: gamma of v wedge w equals gamma v times gamma w, minus φ of v, w. And equation 27 of the paper, not shown, gives the explicit formulas for trivectors and four-vectors.

Now split φ into a symmetric part g and an antisymmetric part ω. Equation 26 says two things. The anticommutator of two generators is twice g of v, w: it sees only g. And gamma of v wedge w is half the commutator, minus ω of v, w. So ω changes which element of the algebra is called the bivector v wedge w. All of this was checked for a generic, non-symmetric φ.

On the right, two comments. The paper asserts that these conditions determine gamma uniquely, and that the result is a Clifford algebra of the same dimension for every φ. Uniqueness is clear from the recursion. Existence, that an associative gamma exists for all multivectors, is not proved in the paper. It is Chevalley's theorem, from 1954, and Oziewicz himself constructs the product in 1997.

And at the bottom, what the paper hoped for. It invites a reading in terms of Einstein's non-symmetric unified field theory, with g gravitational and ω electromagnetic, and it later suggests that ω distorts the spinor space. The next two slides test those hopes.
