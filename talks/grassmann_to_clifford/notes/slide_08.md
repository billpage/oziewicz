Now the mathematics. The first move of the paper is to define Grassmann's products by evaluation.

Equation 3 says: the value of a wedge product of k covectors on a wedge product of k vectors is the determinant of the matrix of values, α i of x j. This one definition fixes both wedge products at once, of multivectors and of forms. There is no factorial and no metric. Associativity of the wedge is then a theorem about determinants. The paper stresses that this determinant is the value of a form on a multivector, not a scalar product, and contrasts it with Hestenes, and with Hestenes and Sobczyk's book.

Next, exterior multiplication becomes an operator: e of x acting on y is x wedge y. Equation 7, e of x composed with e of y equals e of x wedge y, says that e is an algebra homomorphism. That is the associativity of the wedge, which is a different statement from the trivial associativity of composition.

Contraction is then defined as the transpose: i of x acting on a form ω, evaluated on y, is ω evaluated on x wedge y. And equation 12 says that i of x composed with i of y is i of y wedge x. The order reverses, because transposition reverses products: i is an anti-homomorphism. This little reversal matters later, for a sign in Jadczyk's paper.

On the right, an example: i of x applied to α wedge β gives α of x times β, minus β of x times α. That is the usual left contraction.

And equation 13, the antiderivation rule for contraction. In most textbooks, Greub's Multilinear Algebra for example, this is the definition of contraction. Here it is a consequence of the determinant pairing: it is the Laplace expansion of a determinant. All of these were checked exactly in dimensions three and four.
